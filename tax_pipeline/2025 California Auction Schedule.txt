Understanding "Excess Proceeds"
Since you specifically mentioned proceeds parcels, keep the strict California statutory timeline in mind:
The Debt: Properties become subject to the Tax Collector’s Power to Sell after being tax-delinquent for 5 years (or 3 years for commercial property/vacant land).
The Excess: If a parcel sells at auction for more than the minimum bid (the outstanding back taxes and fees), the remaining balance becomes Excess Proceeds.
The Clock: Parties of interest (the former owner, lienholders) have exactly one year from the date the Tax Collector's deed is officially recorded to file a claim with the county to recover those funds. After one year, the window closes permanently and unclaimed funds go to the county.
If you are looking for the exact parcel list or excess proceeds ledger for a specific area, you just need to check the official website of that specific county's Treasurer-Tax Collector.

User prompt: The state uh recorder or the state treasurer
Response: Neither of them. When it comes to tax sales and excess proceeds, the state never touches the money, and the state treasury or state recorder have nothing to do with it.
In California, the entire process is strictly handled at the county level.
The specific entities that manage this money are:
1. The County Treasurer-Tax Collector
This is the office that holds the actual auction, takes the money, satisfies the back taxes, and holds the remaining excess proceeds in a local county trust fund. You file the excess proceeds claim form directly with them.
2. The County Recorder
The Recorder's office doesn't hold the money, but they control the clock. They are the ones who officially record the "Tax Deed to Purchaser." The day they stamp and record that deed is the exact date the strict 1-year countdown begins for people to claim their money.
3. The County Auditor-Controller
Once the 1-year deadline passes and the Tax Collector approves a claim, the County Auditor-Controller is the office that actually cuts and mails the check to the claimant.
Where the Money Goes if Unclaimed
If no one claims the excess proceeds within that 1-year window, the money does not go to the California State Treasurer or the State Controller's Unclaimed Property division. Instead, the law states it is permanently absorbed directly into the County General Fund.

User prompt: Where's the oversight?
Response: Even though individual counties handle the day-to-day operations and hold the money, they don't get to make up their own rules. The oversight comes directly from the California State Controller's Office (SCO) and strict state laws.
Under California Government Code Section 30301, the State Controller explicitly instructs, advises, and directs all 58 county tax collectors on exactly how they must perform their duties under the law.
The oversight system relies on three specific guardrails:
1. The State Controller’s Mandated Pre-Approval
A county cannot just decide to hold a tax sale on a whim.
Pursuant to California Revenue and Taxation Code (RTC) Section 3700.5, the county tax collector must formally notify the State Controller’s Office at least 45 to 120 days before any proposed public auction.
The county must submit their full list of intended parcels and secure authorization before a single piece of land goes to auction.
2. The SCO "Excess Proceeds Guide" & Reference Manuals
The state enforces uniformity through the County Tax Collector’s Reference Manual and a dedicated Excess Proceeds Guide. These books dictate the exact legal baseline the county must follow, including:
The exact mandatory wording for notifying former owners that they have money waiting for them.
The strict formulas used to determine who gets paid first (e.g., satisfying state tax liens, then municipal liens, then the former homeowner).
3. Legal Remedy (The 90-Day Appeal Rule)
If a county tax collector improperly denies an excess proceeds claim, or sits on it past the legal deadline, the ultimate oversight falls to the California Superior Court system. Under the law, once the county makes an official determination on a claim, any party has 90 days to file a lawsuit in court to challenge the decision or force compliance.
So while the state doesn't hold the cash, they act as the regulatory referee. If a county tax collector steps out of line, they violate state statutory code and answer directly to the State Controller and the courts.

User prompt: How often are they challenged?
Response: Formal lawsuits in Superior Court challenging a county’s decision are actually very rare—occurring in less than 1% to 2% of cases.
Instead of full-blown legal battles, almost all the "challenging" happens during the mandatory one-year waiting window via competing administrative claims handled directly by the county.
The breakdown of how often conflicts happen and why they rarely make it to court follows a specific pattern:
1. The Real "Challenge": Conflicting Claims (~10% to 15% of high-value sales)
True conflict usually arises when multiple people try to claim the exact same pool of money. The county doesn't investigate; they simply collect the applications for a year and sort them by the legal hierarchy established by California Revenue and Taxation Code Section 4675:
First Priority: Lienholders of record (mortgage companies, mechanics liens, state/federal tax liens).
Second Priority: The former owner of record.
If a property has three different old liens and a former owner all submitting paperwork, they are technically "challenging" each other's right to the money. The county Tax Collector analyzes the recording dates, maps out the payout order, and submits a distribution recommendation to the County Board of Supervisors for final approval.
2. Why Court Challenges are So Rare (The 90-Day Rule)
Once the Board of Supervisors signs off on who gets the money, a claimant has exactly 90 days to file a lawsuit in Superior Court to contest it. People rarely do this for three practical reasons:
The Law is Black and White: California's priority system is rigid. If a bank had a valid $50,000 deed of trust recorded before the tax sale, they get paid before the owner. There is very little gray area for an attorney to argue in court.
Cost vs. Reward: Filing a civil lawsuit, paying a retainer, and dragging a county through litigation easily costs thousands of dollars. Unless the excess proceeds amount is massive, the legal fees quickly swallow up the money being fought over.
Asset Recovery Companies: A massive chunk of these claims are filed by professional asset recovery corporations (who find the former owners and file the paperwork for a percentage fee). These companies know the state guidelines inside and out; they rarely submit defective claims that require a court appeal.
The Main Reason Claims Get Rejected
When a claim fails, it is almost never because the county actively fought the claimant. It's usually due to administrative errors:
Missing the 1-Year Deadline: The claim was postmarked even one day late. The county by law cannot accept it.
Poor Documentation: The claimant couldn't provide a valid chain of title, or failed to provide notary signatures and corporate entity proofs showing they actually represent the entity that held the debt.
Unless the county makes a blatant mathematical error or overlooks a recorded document, their administrative distribution is usually the final word.
